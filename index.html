Hardware from design to flight test

I work on the aircraft's hardware across the whole loop. I design, simulate and tolerance parts, then release them for CNC machining, sheet metal, 3D printing and injection moulding. I fly the aircraft, and the next revision starts from what the flight and field tests show. I work through that with the electronics team, and the DMU in 3DEXPERIENCE keeps the configuration under control.

The aircraft works outdoors and around a lot of water. I design the IP67 electronics enclosures and the IP54 structural assemblies: seals, gaskets, drainage, and materials and finishes that hold up in the field. I also designed the battery integration, the pack mounting and the high-current harness routing.

A product designer and I work on packaging, ergonomics and serviceability, so the aircraft stays easy to handle and maintain in the field.

Aiming a water jet from a hovering airframe

The nozzle sits on a two-axis gimbal so the aircraft can hold position and steer the jet. I designed the swivel joints and the servo drive, selected and calibrated the servos, and integrated the gimbal with the flight controller. The jet has to stay coherent over distance, so the flow path and the straightener upstream of the nozzle were part of the same design loop.

Sizing the airframe around water that moves

A partially filled tank does not behave like a fixed mass. During payload flight testing we stepped up the water load until a sloshing-driven instability showed up and forced a safe abort. I analysed the slosh dynamics and the moment of inertia across fill levels, and the tank and airframe interfaces were resized from that data.

Fixing what the field found

Two examples from Mk2 test campaigns. A harness failure traced back to vibration-induced abrasion of a phase cable, fixed with routing and clamping changes. Screws on the battery bay fairing loosened under vibration, so I replaced them with a bayonet lock.

This program is under NDA, so there are no images here. I can go into more detail in conversation.

Battery module and thermal runaway containment

Three generations of a propulsion battery module for a 9-seat electric aircraft, from jigs and fuses on Mark 1 to the full pack architecture on Mark 2. The core problem: one cell goes into thermal runaway, and the neighbours, the casing and the aircraft have to survive it.

Mark 1 and Mark 1+: a module you can actually assemble

I started with the tooling: jigs and fixtures for cell handling and interconnection. The fuse design campaign for the cell interconnects came next, characterising how the interconnects open under fault current.

On Mark 1+ I designed the module itself. Sheet metal casing, the assembly sequence, and the jigs and alignment features that make the interconnection repeatable. The assembly sequence drove the design as much as the loads did.

Mark 2: propagation or containment

Mark 2 was the pack architecture. Where the cells sit, how they mount, how the pack integrates into the aircraft. The central decision was whether to let a thermal runaway propagate in a controlled way or contain it at the cell, and I worked on both sides of that trade.

I took the concepts from design through manufacturing and assembly, wrote the test requests, set up the instrumentation, and did the post-mortem analysis. With the materials and thermal engineers I developed sealing and venting methods and multi-material stack-ups for the module interior: composite protection against thermal ablation and aerogel thermal barriers, sized so they carry the structural loads and can still be manufactured and assembled. External suppliers were part of that loop.

Thesis: one structure for heat, vibration and shock

My thesis was the interstitial structure between the cells. It has to contain a thermal runaway, survive vibration and shock, seal the casing, and stay within mass and packaging. I ran vibration and shock simulations against DO-160G and thermomechanical simulations against DO-311A, then ran the vibration and shock tests on hardware. The sealing of the casing under runaway temperatures was simulated in Abaqus explicit and implicit with temperature-dependent materials past 1000 °C. I built and burned the test articles. The structure is now patent-pending, with me as a named co-inventor.

What the thermal runaway test showed

The test forced fast propagation with several heated cells near the centre of the module. It compared epoxy and phenolic E-glass facesheets, and PU and PES foam cores. The idea behind the material split: the facesheets take little volume but hold the cells, so they have to survive as a charred skeleton. The foam takes most of the volume, so it should burn off with little residue and leave the vent paths open.

Phenolic held together and kept the cell array in place. Epoxy broke up and let the cell cans scatter, which blocks flow paths and pushes the pressure up. PES left less residue than PU. The middle facesheet left a clean ring on each cell at mid-height, so it also acts as a local wrapper. The updated design adds reinforced lugs and cell retainers.

Patent-pending. Most figures on this page are from my bachelor thesis.

Kolibri: fixed-wing eVTOL for defibrillator delivery

A cardiac arrest patient in Germany waits about nine minutes for a defibrillator. Mission Pulse set out to halve that with a drone. Kolibri is the second prototype: a 3 m carbon eVTOL that flies to the patient, hovers, and lowers an AED on a rope. I led aerostructures and CAD, and worked on the aircraft from the first structural layout to the flight line.

OrganisationHORYZN, TUM student initiative
RoleAerostructures & CAD Team Lead
PeriodAug 2023 to Jan 2025
Materials and toolsPrepreg CFRP, Divinycell F core, 3DEXPERIENCE
3 m wingspan
21 kg take-off mass
2 kg AED payload
CFRP sandwich structure

Structural layout and CAD lead

I owned the structural layout of the aircraft: where the loads go, where the bonded joints and fasteners sit, and how the parts split for manufacturing. I ran the design reviews and held the CAD standards across the team on 3DEXPERIENCE, through prototype build and integration.

Before the lead role I was on the software and avionics side, writing path planning and precision landing features in ROS and Python. That is useful now: I know what the flight software needs from the structure.

Molds, wingbox and lamination

The airframe is a sandwich: prepreg carbon skins over a Divinycell F foam core. I designed the molds and tooling for the parts, the wingbox that ties the lift arms and the wing together, and then laminated the parts myself. Designing a laminate and laying it up are two different skills, and doing both changed how I design.

Payload box and rope release

Regulations do not allow the aircraft to land next to bystanders, so the AED goes down on a rope from hover. I designed the payload box that holds the defibrillator and the spool mechanism that pays out the rope and releases the box on the ground. Both live in the fuselage and had to fit around the existing structure.

Flight test

Kolibri flew. Hover, transition and precision landing were tested in the field. Flight test is where the structure gets its real load cases, and where the next revision starts.

Sponsors, livery, funding and presenting

A student aircraft is also a fundraising project. I worked with sponsors on the livery, on funding, on getting a video made, and I presented the aircraft. The engineering does not fly without that side.
